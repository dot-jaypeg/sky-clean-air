Where a mini-split makes the most sense

We install mini-splits in all kinds of homes, but the calls tend to come from the same few situations:

  • Older homes with no ductwork. A lot of 1920s through 1950s houses in North Park, Kensington, Normal Heights and Point Loma were built with a wall heater or a floor furnace and nothing else. Adding ducts means losing closet space or opening ceilings. A mini-split skips all of that.
  • ADUs and granny flats. With so many new ADUs going up across San Diego and Orange County, a mini-split is often the simplest way to give the unit its own heating and cooling, on its own controls.
  • Garage conversions and home gyms. Garages are hard to tie into the main system, and they get brutally hot in Escondido or Lake Forest in August.
  • Room additions. The original system usually wasn't sized for the extra square footage, and extending ducts to a new wing rarely works as well as people hope.
  • The one room that never cooperates. The west-facing bedroom, the upstairs office, the sunroom. Instead of overcooling the whole house to fix one room, a single indoor unit handles it directly.

How a ductless system actually works

A mini-split has two main pieces. The outdoor unit, a compact condenser, sits on a pad or a wall bracket. Inside, one or more air handlers mount on a wall, in the ceiling, or low on the wall near the floor. A small bundle of refrigerant lines, a power cable and a drain line connect them through a hole about three inches across.

Almost every mini-split sold today is a heat pump, so the same system cools in summer and heats on cool nights. Each indoor unit has its own remote or wall control, so the bedroom can be at 68 while the living room sits at 74.

Single-zone or multi-zone?

A single-zone system is one outdoor unit paired with one indoor unit, which is perfect for an ADU, a garage or a single problem room. A multi-zone system runs several indoor units off one outdoor unit, which works well for a small home that has no ducts at all. For bigger homes, it's sometimes better to use two separate systems rather than one large multi-zone. We'll talk through which setup fits your space.

What to decide before install day

These are the things we'll walk through with you during the free estimate:

  • Where the indoor units go. High on the wall is most common and most efficient. Ceiling cassettes look cleaner but need attic access above. We'll avoid spots that blow straight onto a bed or a desk.
  • Where the outdoor unit goes. It needs airflow around it and a reasonable path for the line set. On tight lots in Hillcrest or a condo patio in Irvine, a wall bracket often saves the day.
  • How the lines run. We plan the route so the line set is short and tidy, and can add a paintable line-set cover on the outside wall so it doesn't look like an afterthought.
  • Electrical. Most systems need a dedicated circuit. We'll check your panel and let you know if it needs any work.
  • HOA rules. If you're in a condo or planned community, check whether the HOA has guidelines for outdoor equipment placement. It's easier to know that before we drill.

The installation, step by step

  1. We confirm the placement of each indoor and outdoor unit with you on site.
  2. We mount the indoor unit's bracket and drill the small wall penetration for the lines.
  3. We set the outdoor unit on its pad or bracket.
  4. We run and connect the refrigerant lines, communication wire and condensate drain.
  5. We pull a vacuum on the lines to clear moisture and air, then check for leaks before releasing refrigerant.
  6. We connect power, start the system, and test cooling and heating in each zone.
  7. We show you how to use the controls and how to clean the filters, which is the one maintenance task you'll do yourself.

A single-zone install is usually a one-day job. Multi-zone systems and installs that need electrical upgrades can take a bit longer.

The honest trade-offs

We like mini-splits, but they're not the right answer for everyone. A few things to know going in:

  • You'll see the indoor unit. Wall units are fairly slim, but they're visible. Ceiling cassettes and floor units are options if that bothers you.
  • Filters need regular cleaning. Every indoor unit has washable filters. In homes with pets, or during wildfire smoke and Santa Ana dust, they load up faster.
  • Whole-house cost can climb. If your home already has good ductwork, central air or a ducted heat pump is often less expensive than putting a mini-split in every room.
  • Coastal homes need a little extra care. Salt air from Ocean Beach to San Clemente is hard on outdoor coils. Placement away from direct spray and an occasional rinse help them last.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do mini-splits heat as well as cool?

Nearly all modern mini-splits are heat pumps, so yes. For San Diego and Orange County winters, a properly sized unit handles heating comfortably.

How big a hole do you need to make in the wall?

Usually around three inches across, per indoor unit. It's sealed around the line set and covered on the outside.

Can one outdoor unit run several rooms?

Yes, that's a multi-zone system. Depending on the model, one outdoor unit can serve several indoor units. We'll help you decide whether one multi-zone or two separate systems makes more sense.

Is a mini-split a good choice for an ADU?

It's one of the most common choices. It gives the ADU its own heating and cooling and its own controls, without tying into the main house.

How long does installation take?

Most single-zone installs are done in a day. Multi-zone systems or jobs that need a new electrical circuit can take longer, and we'll give you the timeline with your estimate.

Is a mini-split cheaper than central air?

For one or two rooms, usually. For a whole house that already has good ducts, central air or a ducted heat pump is often the better value. We're happy to price both.
