"Should I fix it or replace it?"

It's the question we hear most at kitchen tables from Chula Vista to Brea, usually right after a tech has delivered some bad news. Nobody wants to spend thousands on a new system if the old one has years left. Nobody wants to pour money into repairs on something that's about to quit, either.

There's no single right answer. But there are a few honest ways to think it through, and that's what this page is for.

About that 15-year number

You'll hear that HVAC systems last around 15 years. It's a reasonable rule of thumb, and it's the point where our techs start paying closer attention. When we're in your home for duct cleaning, dryer vent work or insulation, we look over the whole system, and if it's in that range we'll mention it.

But 15 years is a checkpoint, not an expiration date. We've seen well-kept systems in mild coastal neighborhoods still running reliably past that mark. We've also seen 10-year-old units in hot inland attics in Santee or Lake Forest that are worn out. Age is one input. It isn't the decision.

When repair usually makes more sense

  • The failure is a single, common part, such as a capacitor, contactor, igniter, fan motor or a clogged drain line, and the rest of the system is in decent shape.
  • The system is on the younger side and this is the first real problem it's had.
  • It still keeps the house comfortable and your bills haven't been climbing.
  • You're planning a move soon and just need reliable heating and cooling until then.

In those cases, we'll tell you to fix it. Our HVAC repair page covers what that visit looks like.

When replacement starts to win

  • The repair is major, like a failed compressor or a leaking evaporator coil, on a system that's already getting old.
  • You're calling for repairs every season, and the bills are adding up to a real chunk of a new system's cost.
  • The system uses R-22 refrigerant. It's been phased out, so recharging an older R-22 system is expensive and getting harder.
  • It can't keep up anymore. It runs all afternoon, some rooms never get comfortable, and the energy bill keeps rising.
  • Your home has changed. An addition, a garage conversion or a big remodel may mean the old system was never sized for the house you live in now.

If two or three of these sound familiar, replacement is worth a serious look.

A simple way to weigh it

Here's the kind of math we walk through with homeowners:

  1. What does this repair cost? We'll give you a written price.
  2. What's likely next? Based on the system's age and condition, is this probably a one-off, or the first of several?
  3. What would replacement cost? We'll price that too, with options.
  4. How long are you staying? A new system pays off over years. If you're selling next spring, that changes things.

Then it's your call. We give you both numbers side by side, with no pressure either way.

Local things that wear systems out faster

San Diego and Orange County are easy on people and harder on HVAC equipment than you'd think. Near the water in Point Loma, La Jolla or Dana Point, salt air corrodes outdoor coils and electrical parts. In the hotter valleys, including El Cajon, Escondido, Poway and Mission Viejo, systems run long cycles for weeks at a time in late summer. Santa Ana winds and wildfire smoke clog filters and coils. And in a lot of older 1950s–70s homes in Clairemont and North Park, the equipment and ducts sit in attics that reach well over outdoor temperatures by mid-afternoon.

That's why we look at more than the nameplate date. A 12-year-old unit's condition depends a lot on where it's lived.

Frequently Asked Questions

My system is 15 years old. Do I have to replace it?

No. Fifteen years is a good time to start planning, not a deadline. If it's running well, keeping the house comfortable and not costing you in repairs, a tune-up and regular care may keep it going for a while.

Can I replace just the outdoor unit?

Sometimes, but indoor and outdoor components are designed to work as a matched pair. Mixing a new outdoor unit with an old indoor coil can hurt efficiency and reliability. We'll explain what applies to your specific system.

Will a new system lower my energy bills?

Newer equipment is generally more efficient than a system from 15 or 20 years ago, especially if it's sized correctly and the ducts aren't leaking. How much you save depends on your home and how you use it, so we won't promise a specific number.

How long does a replacement take?

Many straightforward replacements take a day or two. If we're changing system types or reworking ductwork, it takes longer. You'll get a timeline with your estimate.
