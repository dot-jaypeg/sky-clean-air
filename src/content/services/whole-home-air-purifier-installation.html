If you own three portable purifiers, read this

A lot of the homeowners who ask us about whole-home purification already have a portable unit in the bedroom, another in the living room and maybe one in the kid's room. They work, in the room they're in. But they're loud on the high setting, the filters add up, and the hallway, kitchen and office aren't getting any of it.

A whole-home air purifier takes a different approach. It's installed at your heating and air conditioning system, usually where air returns to the air handler, so every bit of air your system moves gets filtered before it goes back out through the vents. If the ducts reach a room, the purifier does too.

What's actually inside the cabinet

"Whole-home air purifier" is a broad term. The equipment we install most often falls into a few categories:

  • High-efficiency media filters. A thick, pleated filter in a dedicated cabinet, often several inches deep instead of the 1-inch filter behind your return grille. The extra depth means more surface area, so it can catch fine particles like smoke and pollen without choking the airflow. For many homes this is the workhorse. See media filter installation.
  • Carbon or odor-control filtration. Activated carbon helps with cooking smells, pet odors and some smoke odor. It's often paired with a particle filter rather than used alone.
  • UV lights. These target microbial growth on the AC coil rather than particles in the air. They're a good add-on for musty-smell problems but not a substitute for filtration. More on our UV air purifier page.

We stay away from equipment that generates ozone. Ozone is a lung irritant, and adding it to your home's air isn't our idea of purification.

Where it helps most in Southern California

Wildfire smoke. Smoke is mostly very fine particles, and they come in through doors, windows and gaps no matter how tightly you close up. During a smoke event, a home with good central filtration and the fan running is in a much better position than one with a basic filter. Neighborhoods in the East County and North County inland areas, like Alpine, Ramona, Poway and Escondido, tend to feel it first, but Santa Ana winds can push smoke to the coast and across Orange County too.

Pets and allergies. Dander, pollen and dust mite debris get stirred up and cycled through your system all day. A better filter pulls more of it out of circulation. You'll often notice it first as less dust settling on shelves and ceiling fans.

Remodel and construction dust. If you're living in a house mid-remodel, or next to new construction, fine dust gets everywhere. Filtration helps, although you'll want to protect the system and ducts during heavy dust work too.

Freeway-adjacent homes. Plenty of neighborhoods sit close to the 5, 15, 805 or the 405, and fine particles from traffic find their way inside.

What a purifier won't do

It's worth setting expectations. A central purifier only cleans air while your HVAC fan is running. If the system sits idle on a mild spring day, the filter isn't doing anything. Many homeowners run the fan more often, or on a circulate setting, during smoke events or allergy season, and a modern thermostat makes that easy.

It also won't fix humidity problems, and it won't clean years of buildup out of your ductwork. If your ducts are heavily soiled, duct cleaning first is often the better order. And if someone in your household has serious respiratory issues, a purifier is a helpful tool, not medical treatment.

Can your system handle it?

This is where installation matters. A high-efficiency filter catches more because it's denser. If you jam a dense filter into a system that wasn't set up for it, you restrict airflow, and that can make the system less efficient, cause coils to freeze, or shorten equipment life.

Before we recommend anything, a tech looks at:

  • The size and condition of your return duct and filter location
  • Your blower and how much airflow the system is moving
  • Where there's room to mount a filter cabinet, whether that's in the attic, garage, closet or under the house
  • The age and condition of the system itself

If your HVAC is past about 15 years old and struggling, we'll tell you honestly whether it's worth adding equipment to or better to plan for replacement first.

Installation, step by step

  1. Talk and look. We ask what's bothering you (smoke, allergies, dust, odors) and inspect your system, return ducts and available space.
  2. Recommend and price. You get a specific recommendation and a written, upfront price before any work starts.
  3. Install the cabinet. We mount the purifier housing in the return side of the system, cut it into the ductwork, and seal the connections so air doesn't sneak around the filter.
  4. Test. We run the system and check airflow to make sure the new filter isn't choking it.
  5. Show you the routine. You'll know where the filter is, how to check it, and roughly how often to change it.

Most installs take a few hours. You'll be without heat or air during part of that time, which is rarely a problem on a mild San Diego day.

Keeping it working

A purifier is only as good as its filter. Media filters generally last longer than a standard 1-inch filter, but how long depends on your home. A house with two dogs in dusty Santee will load a filter faster than a quiet condo in La Jolla. After a heavy smoke event, it's worth checking early. We can also swap filters during your regular maintenance visit.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a whole-home air purifier better than a portable one?

For whole-house coverage, usually yes. A central system filters air for every room on your ductwork and runs quietly out of sight. Portable units still make sense for a specific room, like a bedroom during a bad smoke week, and many people keep one for that.

Does it help with wildfire smoke?

Yes. Smoke is made of fine particles, and a high-efficiency filter captures a large share of them as air passes through. It works best with the HVAC fan running and the house closed up.

Will it make my system work harder?

Not if it's sized and installed correctly. That's why we check your return ducts and airflow first. A deep media filter in a proper cabinet can actually restrict airflow less than a dense 1-inch filter.

How often do the filters need changing?

It varies with the filter type, pets, dust and how often the system runs. We'll give you a starting schedule for your home, and it's worth checking sooner after smoke events or remodel work.

Can I add a purifier to an older system?

Often, yes. If the system is in decent shape and there's room to install the cabinet, it's a straightforward upgrade. If your system is near the end of its life, we'll tell you so, and you can decide whether to add it now or with new equipment.
