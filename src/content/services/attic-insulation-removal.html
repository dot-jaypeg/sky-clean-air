It usually starts with a noise in the ceiling

A lot of our insulation removal calls begin the same way. Someone hears scratching above the bedroom around 2 a.m., or notices a stale, sharp smell in the hallway on a warm afternoon. Sometimes it's a home inspector's report that says "evidence of rodent activity in attic." Other times it's quieter: the upstairs is always hot, the bills keep creeping up, and when someone finally pokes their head through the hatch, the insulation looks like old gray felt.

Whatever brought you here, attic insulation removal means exactly what it sounds like. We vacuum out the old material, clean up what's underneath, and leave you with a bare, sanitized attic that's ready for new insulation.

What we typically find in San Diego and Orange County attics

Every attic is different, but a few situations come up again and again:

  • Rodent contamination. Roof rats love warm attics, especially in older neighborhoods with mature trees and palms like North Park, Mission Hills and Point Loma. They tunnel through insulation, nest in it, and leave droppings and urine throughout.
  • Compressed, worn-out insulation. Insulation works by trapping air. After decades of settling, foot traffic from past repairs, and a layer of dust on top, it gets flat and loses most of its value. This is common in the 1950s–70s homes around Clairemont, Allied Gardens and La Mesa, and in plenty of 1970s–90s tract homes across south Orange County.
  • Water damage. A slow leak around a tile roof valley or a vent can soak insulation, which then clumps, sags and can grow mold. Even after the roof is fixed, the damaged insulation stays behind.
  • Smoke and debris. After a nearby wildfire or a big Santa Ana event, fine ash and dust can settle into attic insulation through the vents. Remodels and solar installs also leave behind construction debris and trampled spots.

Is contaminated insulation actually a health risk?

We want to be straight with you here, because this is a topic where a lot of companies lean on fear.

Rodent droppings, urine and nesting material are not something you want in your home's air. They're a known allergen and asthma trigger, and rodent waste can carry diseases. The attic isn't sealed off from your living space either. Air moves through gaps around light fixtures, the hatch and wiring holes, and if your ductwork runs through the attic, any leaks in it can pull attic air into the system.

That said, most families living under a rodent-affected attic aren't in immediate danger. The realistic concerns are ongoing odor, allergy and asthma flare-ups, and the fact that rodents keep coming back to a spot that smells like other rodents. It's worth dealing with properly. It's not a reason to panic.

The bigger safety point is about who does the cleanup. Stirring up dried droppings without protection is how people actually get exposed, which is why we don't recommend doing it yourself with a shop vac and a dust mask. Our crew works in protective suits and respirators, and the waste goes straight into sealed bags outside.

One more honest note: in some older homes, we occasionally find loose vermiculite insulation, the pebbly, gray-brown kind. Some vermiculite contains asbestos. If we see it, we stop and recommend testing before anything is disturbed.

Removal, or just add more on top?

Not every attic needs a full tear-out. If your insulation is dry, clean, free of droppings and just too thin, adding new insulation over it is often the sensible, lower-cost choice, and we'll tell you so.

Removal makes sense when the existing material is contaminated, wet, moldy or so compressed that it's getting in the way. Covering rodent waste with fresh insulation just traps the smell and gives the next rodent a nicer nest. It also makes air sealing nearly impossible, since the gaps you need to seal are buried under the old material.

What removal day looks like

  1. Inspection and photos. We go up first, check the depth and condition of the insulation, look for droppings, moisture and damaged wiring, and photograph what we find. You get a written price before any work starts. If you'd like this as a standalone visit, book an attic inspection.
  2. Protecting your home. We cover the floor along the path to the attic access and set up containment around the hatch so dust stays where it belongs.
  3. Checking for hazards. Before removing anything, we look for exposed wiring, older recessed lights and anything else we need to work carefully around.
  4. Vacuum removal. A high-powered, HEPA-filtered insulation vacuum pulls loose fill out of the attic through a hose to a sealed collection bag outside. Batts are bagged by hand.
  5. Cleanup and sanitizing. Once the insulation is out, we remove nests and droppings, vacuum the joists and attic floor, and apply a sanitizer to cut the odor and the scent trails that draw rodents back.
  6. Walkthrough. We show you before-and-after photos and talk through next steps, whether that's new insulation right away or after other repairs are done.

Most homes are done in a day, though larger attics or heavy contamination can take longer. You can usually stay home the whole time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I stay home while you remove the insulation?

In most cases, yes. The work area is contained at the attic access, and the removed material goes out through a hose to a bag outside. If anyone in the house has severe asthma or a compromised immune system, you might prefer to be out for the day, and we're happy to talk that through.

How long does attic insulation removal take?

Most single-family homes take one day. Very large attics, tight crawl spaces or heavy rodent contamination can add time. We'll give you an estimate of the schedule along with the price.

Should I get new insulation right after removal?

Usually, yes. A bare attic lets a lot of heat into the house, especially in summer. Many homeowners schedule new insulation right after removal. If there's a roof repair or rodent issue to handle first, it's better to wait until that's done.

Is it safe to remove rodent-contaminated insulation myself?

We don't recommend it. Disturbing dried droppings and nesting material without proper protection is how people get exposed, and a household vacuum can blow fine particles back into the air. Removal also generates a lot of bulky waste that needs proper bagging and disposal.

Will removing old insulation get rid of the smell?

Removing the contaminated material and sanitizing the surfaces takes care of the source, which is where nearly all of the odor comes from. If the smell has also made its way into your ductwork, duct cleaning may help with what's left.

What if you find something like asbestos?

If we see vermiculite or any material that looks like it could contain asbestos, we stop work and recommend testing through a qualified lab before anything is disturbed. We'd rather lose a day than put your family or our crew at risk.
