One machine, two jobs

A heat pump looks almost exactly like a central air conditioner. The outdoor unit sits in the side yard, lines run to a coil or air handler inside, and air moves through your ducts. The difference is a valve that lets it run in reverse. In summer it pulls heat out of your house and dumps it outside. In winter it pulls heat from the outdoor air, even cool air, and brings it inside.

Because it's moving heat rather than burning fuel to create it, a heat pump can deliver a lot of warmth for the electricity it uses. And it replaces two pieces of equipment, your AC and your furnace, with one.

Why they suit San Diego and Orange County so well

Heat pumps lose some efficiency when it's truly cold outside, which is why they took longer to catch on in places with hard winters. We don't have that problem. Even in the colder inland spots like Ramona, Santee or Escondido, winter nights here are mild compared with most of the country, and a modern, properly sized heat pump handles them comfortably.

Meanwhile, summers keep getting hotter, especially in El Cajon, Poway, Mission Viejo and Lake Forest. A heat pump is a full air conditioner for those months. For a lot of households the result is one system that runs efficiently all year instead of a furnace that sits idle for eight months and an AC that sits idle for the other four.

If you have rooftop solar, a heat pump also lets your heating run on electricity instead of gas, which is a big part of why many solar homeowners ask about them.

Is your home a good fit?

A heat pump is worth a close look if any of these sound like you:

  • Your AC and your furnace are both getting old, and you're facing two replacements anyway
  • You don't have air conditioning yet and want to add it
  • You have solar, or plan to, and want to move off gas
  • Your home is all-electric already, or has no gas line where the equipment would go
  • You're adding an ADU or addition that needs its own heating and cooling

And it may not be the right move if your furnace is only a few years old and working well. In that case, replacing just the AC often makes more financial sense. We'll tell you that directly.

Ducted or ductless

Ducted heat pumps connect to your existing ductwork and heat and cool the whole home, the same way central air does. This is the usual choice for tract homes across Irvine, Rancho Bernardo and Carlsbad that already have ducts.

Ductless heat pumps, better known as mini-splits, mount directly in the rooms they serve. They're a great option for older homes in North Park or Point Loma that never had ducts, and for garages, ADUs and additions. See our mini-split installation page for more on that route.

What we look at before recommending one

  • The size of the house and how it holds heat. Insulation, windows, sun exposure and ceiling height all change what size system you need. If your attic insulation is thin, improving it can let you buy a smaller system. See attic insulation.
  • Your ducts. Leaky or undersized ducts rob any system of performance, and heat pumps deliver air at a gentler temperature than a furnace, so good airflow matters more. We check the ductwork and tell you if it needs work.
  • Your electrical panel. Moving from gas heat to a heat pump adds electrical load. We'll check whether your panel has room, and let you know early if it doesn't.
  • Your microclimate. Coastal homes near Del Mar or San Clemente need a unit placed and protected with salt air in mind. Inland homes need enough capacity for long, hot afternoons.

Install day

For a typical ducted system replacing an old furnace and AC, the work looks like this. We remove the old equipment and recover the refrigerant. We install the new indoor air handler or coil and the outdoor heat pump, connect and seal the refrigerant lines, the electrical and the condensate drain, and tie the new equipment into your ducts. If your old furnace was gas, the gas line is capped safely. Then we set up your thermostat for heat pump operation, test heating and cooling, and walk you through how it works.

Most replacements are completed in one day, sometimes two if duct or electrical work is part of the job.

What to expect once it's running

A heat pump feels a bit different from a furnace. The air from the vents is warm, but not as hot as a gas furnace blast, and the system tends to run longer at a steady pace instead of short, hot bursts. The house stays at a more even temperature. It's normal, and most people come to prefer it.

On cold mornings you may also notice the outdoor unit go quiet for a few minutes and give off a little steam. That's a defrost cycle, and it's also normal.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will a heat pump keep my house warm enough?

In San Diego and Orange County, yes. Our winters are mild, and a properly sized modern heat pump heats comfortably even on the coldest inland nights we get.

Can a heat pump replace both my AC and my furnace?

Yes. That's one of the biggest reasons people choose one. A single system handles both cooling and heating, with an indoor air handler taking the place of the furnace.

Can I use my existing ductwork?

Usually, if it's in good condition and sized correctly. We'll inspect the ducts during your estimate and let you know if anything needs to be sealed, repaired or replaced.

Are heat pumps noisy?

Modern outdoor units are generally about as quiet as a current central AC, and many variable-speed models are quieter because they run at low speed most of the time.

Are there rebates for heat pumps?

There often are, but programs and amounts change and depend on your utility and home. We'll talk through what may apply during your estimate. Always check the program's current terms before relying on it.

How long does a heat pump last?

Lifespan depends on the equipment, how hard it works and how well it's maintained. Coastal salt air and heavy inland use both take a toll. Annual maintenance is the best way to get the most years out of it.
