You've tried the candles. You've changed the filter. You've opened the windows on a nice day. And still, the first blast of air from the vents smells like a damp towel, or old smoke, or the dog. That's one of the most common calls we get, and it usually means the smell is living on the inside surfaces of your ductwork.

Duct sanitizing is how we go after it. Here's how it works, when it's worth it, and when it isn't.

Isn't that the same as duct cleaning?

Not quite. Air duct cleaning is mechanical: brushes, air whips and a HEPA-filtered vacuum pull dust and debris out of the system. Sanitizing is a treatment applied after cleaning, meant to reduce bacteria, mold spores and the odor they cause on the surfaces inside the ducts.

The order matters. Spraying a sanitizer into dirty ducts doesn't accomplish much, because the product lands on a layer of dust instead of the duct wall. So every sanitizing job we do starts with a full cleaning. If you've already had your ducts cleaned recently and the smell stuck around, tell us, and we'll factor that in.

Who actually needs it?

Most homes don't. For ordinary dust, cleaning is enough. Sanitizing earns its place when something specific has happened:

  • A musty or sour smell when the system starts, especially in the morning
  • Smoke odor that hangs on after a wildfire week or from a previous owner who smoked
  • Pet odor that comes back no matter how often you clean the house
  • A water leak, roof leak or AC drain overflow near the air handler or ducts
  • Rodent or pest activity in the attic or crawlspace
  • A rental unit between tenants, or a home you just bought and want to start fresh in

If it's a pest problem, we'll also look at whether any ducts were chewed or contaminated badly enough that sections should be replaced instead. Treating a damaged duct doesn't fix it.

What does the treatment involve?

First, the cleaning

We protect your floors and furniture, set up negative pressure with our HEPA vacuum, and work every supply run, return and register. We'll clean the blower area if it's accessible, since that's a common place for odor-causing buildup to sit.

Then, the sanitizer

We apply a sanitizing solution as a fine mist through the system so it reaches the inside surfaces of the ducts. Your tech focuses on the spots where the smell is strongest, which is why it helps if you can tell us which rooms and what time of day it's worst. The product gets the contact time it needs to work.

During application and for a short time after, we may ask people and pets to spend a little time in another part of the house. Your tech will tell you how long based on the size of your home.

Finally, a fresh-air run

We run the fan to move air through the clean system, check airflow at the vents, put everything back and walk through the house with you. The goal is air that smells like nothing, not air that smells like a perfume covering something else.

Why does this come up so much here?

A lot of it is the coast. In Pacific Beach, Point Loma, La Jolla and down through Imperial Beach, the marine layer keeps things damp for long stretches, and ducts running through a cool, moist crawlspace or attic can develop that faint mildew smell. The same goes for coastal Orange County, from Newport Beach down to San Clemente.

Inland, the problem is more often smoke. When Santa Ana winds push wildfire smoke across East County, homes in El Cajon, Santee, Lakeside and Ramona can hold onto that smell in the ductwork well after the air outside clears. Fine smoke particles settle on duct surfaces, and every time the system runs, you smell it again.

Then there's age. Older homes in North Park, Clairemont and Golden Hill, and plenty of 1970s–80s houses in Irvine and Lake Forest, have ductwork that's been collecting whatever's come through the house for decades.

What if sanitizing isn't the answer?

Sometimes the smell isn't coming from the ducts at all. It could be a clogged condensate drain, a dirty evaporator coil, or a leak that's letting damp attic air into the return. If your tech finds one of those, we'll tell you, because sanitizing a duct won't fix a drain line. Coil and drain problems are usually handled through AC cleaning, and leaky ducts through duct sealing.

For ongoing air quality concerns beyond a one-time odor, you may be better served by something that runs every day, like a UV air purifier or a better filter. We'll talk through the options without pushing any of them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the sanitizer safe for kids and pets?

The products we use are meant for this application and are safe when applied as directed. We may ask people and pets to stay out of the treated area for a short time during and after application, and your tech will tell you when it's fine to go back in.

Will sanitizing get rid of mold?

It can reduce mold spores and the odor they produce on duct surfaces. If there's active mold growth caused by an ongoing moisture problem, the moisture has to be fixed first, or it will come back. We'll tell you if that's what we're seeing.

Do I need sanitizing every time I clean my ducts?

No. Most cleanings don't need it. We recommend it when there's a specific odor, water event, smoke exposure or pest issue.

How long does the smell take to go away?

Most people notice the difference the same day. If some odor lingers, let us know, since it may point to a source outside the ducts, like the coil or drain pan.

Can you sanitize ducts in a rental or commercial space?

Yes. We work with homeowners, landlords and property managers, and we handle commercial spaces too. Unit turnovers between tenants are a common reason people call.

What's the difference between sanitizing and disinfecting?

The terms overlap and people use them loosely. We also offer air duct disinfecting. Your tech can explain which treatment fits your situation.
