Before you call: three things worth checking

Some AC "breakdowns" take thirty seconds to solve, and we'd rather you not pay for a visit you didn't need. Before you pick up the phone, try these:

  1. The thermostat. Make sure it's set to Cool, the set temperature is below the room temperature, and the screen isn't blank. A blank screen often just means dead batteries.
  2. The breaker. Check the panel for a tripped breaker labeled AC, condenser or furnace. If it's tripped, you can reset it once. If it trips again, leave it off and call us, because something is drawing too much power.
  3. The filter. A filter packed with dust can choke airflow badly enough to freeze the coil. If yours is gray and matted, swap it and give the system a few hours.

Still warm air? Then it's time for a technician.

What your AC is trying to tell you

Most repair calls start with one of a handful of symptoms. Here's what each one usually points to, so you have some idea what to expect.

It runs, but the air isn't cold

Often a failed capacitor on the outdoor unit, a condenser coil caked with dirt, or low refrigerant. Low refrigerant means there's a leak somewhere, and topping it off without finding that leak just delays the next call.

Ice on the copper lines or indoor coil

Almost always an airflow problem or a refrigerant problem. Turn the system off and let it thaw before we arrive, or switch it to Fan only. Running a frozen system can damage the compressor, which is the most expensive part of the unit.

Water where it shouldn't be

A clogged condensate drain is one of the most common calls we get, and one of the most overlooked. In a lot of San Diego and Orange County homes the air handler sits in the attic, so a plugged drain line shows up as a stain on the ceiling below. Catching it early saves drywall.

Clicking, buzzing or grinding

Clicking with no start-up can be a bad contactor or capacitor. Grinding or squealing usually means a motor bearing is going. Buzzing from the outdoor unit with the fan not spinning is worth shutting off until it's looked at.

It turns on and off every few minutes

Short cycling can come from an oversized system, a failing sensor, a thermostat in a bad spot, or a refrigerant issue. It wears parts out quickly and never really dehumidifies the house.

What happens when our tech arrives

We don't guess and start replacing parts. A typical repair visit goes like this:

  1. We ask what you've noticed: when it started, what it sounds like, which rooms are worst.
  2. We check the thermostat, filter and breaker so nothing simple gets missed.
  3. At the outdoor unit we test the capacitor, contactor and fan motor, look at the condenser coil, and check refrigerant pressures and temperatures.
  4. Inside, we look at the evaporator coil, blower, drain line and drain pan.
  5. We explain what we found in plain language and give you a written, upfront price for the repair before any work begins.
  6. Once you approve, we make the repair, run the system, and confirm it's actually cooling at the registers.

If we notice something else along the way, like a duct that's come loose in the attic or a system that's getting close to the end of its life, we'll tell you. You decide what to do about it.

Why local conditions matter for AC repair

Where you live changes what tends to break. Near the coast in Point Loma, Oceanside or Dana Point, salt air corrodes outdoor coils and electrical connections faster than people expect. We see pitted fins and green, crusty terminals on units that aren't that old.

Inland, it's the heat. When El Cajon, Poway or Lake Forest hit the high 90s, systems run almost nonstop, and weak capacitors and tired compressors give out on the hottest afternoon of the year. Attics in those areas can get far hotter than the outdoors, which is hard on air handlers and duct connections.

And during Santa Ana wind events, filters load up with dust and smoke in days instead of months. If your AC started struggling right after a windy week, a filter check is the first thing we'll look at.

What AC repair costs, honestly

We can't quote a repair over the phone without seeing the system, and we'd be suspicious of anyone who does. A worn capacitor and a failed compressor are both "my AC isn't cooling," and they're very different jobs.

What we can promise is that you'll see a written price before we start, and you won't find surprise line items afterward. If a repair doesn't make financial sense on an older system, we'll say so and show you the numbers on both repair and replacement. Not every 15-year-old unit needs to go, but it's worth knowing where yours stands.

The one thing that almost always costs more is waiting. A capacitor that's failing today can take out the compressor next week.

AC Repair: Frequently Asked Questions

Can you come out today?

Often, yes. Same-day and next-day appointments are frequently available, though during heat waves the schedule fills fast. Calling in the morning gives you the best shot at a same-day visit.

Should I keep running my AC if it's blowing warm air?

If you see ice on the lines, hear grinding, or the outdoor fan isn't spinning, turn it off. Running it in those conditions can turn a small repair into a compressor replacement. If it's just not keeping up on a very hot day, it's usually fine to run while you wait for us.

My AC just needs more refrigerant, right?

Refrigerant doesn't get used up. If a system is low, it has a leak. We'll find where it's leaking and give you options, because refilling without a fix means you'll be paying for the same thing again.

Do you repair ductless mini-splits too?

Yes. We work on central air, heat pumps and ductless systems. For ductless units, see our mini-split service page.

How do I know if I should repair or replace?

We look at the age of the unit, its repair history, the refrigerant it uses and the size of the repair. If replacement makes more sense, we'll explain why and still give you the repair price so you can compare.

Do you work on commercial AC systems?

Yes. We handle both residential and commercial cooling across San Diego and Orange County.
