Furnace Repair in San Diego & Orange County

Most furnace problems come down to a handful of parts. We find the one that failed, fix it, and make sure your system is running safely.

First, the safety part

We'll get to repairs in a moment, but a gas furnace is a combustion appliance, so it's worth starting here.

  • If you smell gas (that rotten-egg smell), don't flip switches or try to relight anything. Leave the house and call your gas utility or 911 from outside.
  • If your carbon monoxide alarm goes off, get everyone out, including pets, and call 911. Don't go back in to open windows or turn the furnace off.
  • If people in the house have headaches, dizziness or nausea that get better when they leave, treat it as a possible carbon monoxide problem and get fresh air right away.

Once the emergency side is handled, call us and we'll come find out what went wrong.

What your furnace is doing, and what it usually means

The blower runs, but the air is cold

The furnace is trying to light and failing. Common causes are a worn hot-surface igniter, a dirty flame sensor that shuts the burners off a few seconds after they light, or a gas supply issue. A dirty flame sensor is one of the most frequent and least expensive fixes we make.

It clicks, tries to start, then gives up

Modern furnaces run a series of safety checks before they fire. If one fails, such as a pressure switch that isn't closing because the vent or drain is blocked, the furnace locks itself out. That's the system doing its job, and we'll track down which check it's failing.

It shuts off after a few minutes

Often the furnace is overheating and the high-limit switch is cutting it off. The number one cause is a clogged filter. Closed or blocked vents and a weak blower motor are close behind.

Squealing, rumbling or banging

Squeals usually point to the blower motor or its belt. A loud bang at start-up can mean gas is building up before it lights, which needs attention soon.

Some rooms are warm, others aren't

Sometimes that's the furnace. More often it's the ducts. In attic installs, a disconnected or crushed duct can dump heated air into the attic instead of the back bedroom.

Carbon monoxide: what we check and why it matters

Carbon monoxide is a colorless, odorless gas produced when fuel burns. A healthy furnace sends it safely out through the flue. Problems start when the venting is blocked or disconnected, the burners aren't burning cleanly, or, in older furnaces, the heat exchanger develops a crack.

On every furnace repair, our techs look at the flame, the venting and the heat exchanger area as part of the visit. If we see something that concerns us, we'll show you what we found and explain it plainly. We won't use the words "cracked heat exchanger" as a sales tactic. If there's a real problem, you'll see it for yourself.

No inspection replaces a working CO alarm. California requires them in most homes that have gas appliances or an attached garage, and they belong outside every sleeping area. If yours are more than a few years old, check the date on the back and test them.

How we handle a furnace repair visit

  1. We listen first. What's it doing, when did it start, has anything changed in the house?
  2. We check the basics. Thermostat, filter, power switch and gas valve. You'd be surprised how often a furnace is off because someone flipped the switch that looks like a light switch in the closet.
  3. We read the error code. Most furnaces from the last few decades blink a code on the control board that narrows things down.
  4. We test the parts. Igniter, flame sensor, pressure switches, limit switch, inducer motor, blower and control board, as needed.
  5. We look at combustion and venting. Flame quality, flue connections and the heat exchanger area.
  6. We give you a written price for the repair before we start anything.
  7. We fix it and run a full heating cycle to make sure it lights, stays lit and shuts down properly.

San Diego and Orange County furnaces have their own habits

Because heating season is short here, furnaces sit idle for months. Dust settles on the burners, spiders nest in the burner tubes, and the first cold night in November becomes a very busy day for us. Running your heat briefly in October, before you need it, is a good way to find problems early.

Where the furnace lives matters too. In many 1950s and 1960s homes in Clairemont, Allied Gardens and La Mesa, it's in a hall closet or wall. In a lot of newer tract homes in Rancho Bernardo, Irvine and Lake Forest, it's in the attic or garage, where summer heat and dust take a toll on wiring and the blower. Inland areas like Santee, Ramona and Mission Viejo see colder nights, so those furnaces work harder and earlier in the season.

Repair it or replace it?

Most furnace calls end with a repair, not a replacement. An igniter, flame sensor or capacitor can put a 12-year-old furnace back to work for years. Where we start talking about replacement is when the furnace is well past 15 years, repairs are becoming routine, or there's a safety problem that isn't worth fixing on old equipment.

If we get there, you'll see both options in writing so you can compare. If you'd like to look at a new system, see furnace installation, or consider a heat pump if your AC is aging too.

Furnace Repair: Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my furnace blowing cold air?

If the thermostat is set to Heat and the fan is on Auto, cold air usually means the burners aren't lighting or aren't staying lit. A dirty flame sensor or worn igniter is the most common cause. Check your filter first, then give us a call.

Can a dirty filter really stop my furnace?

Yes. A clogged filter restricts airflow, the furnace overheats, and a safety switch shuts the burners off. Replace the filter and see if it helps. If it keeps shutting off, there's another problem.

How do I know if my furnace is leaking carbon monoxide?

You can't tell by smell or sight, which is why CO alarms matter. Warning signs include headaches, dizziness or nausea that improve when you leave the house, or an alarm going off. If you suspect a leak, get outside and call 911. For peace of mind, our techs check venting and combustion during every furnace repair.

Is it safe to keep using my furnace if it's making a loud bang?

A bang at start-up often means delayed ignition, where gas builds up before it lights. It's best to turn the heat off and have it checked soon.

Do you repair heat pumps too?

Yes. If your heat comes from a heat pump instead of a gas furnace, see our heat pump repair page.

How much does furnace repair cost?

It depends on the part and the furnace. Some fixes are small, others aren't. We'll diagnose the problem and give you a written, upfront price before any work starts.
